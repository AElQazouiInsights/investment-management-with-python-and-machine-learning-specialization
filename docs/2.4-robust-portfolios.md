# Robust Portfolio Construction: Diversification and Risk Budgets

[Covariance estimation](./2.2-covariance.md) addressed noisy risk inputs, and [Black–Litterman](./2.3-black-litterman.md) combined reference-implied means with investor views. We now examine portfolio rules that rely less directly on expected-return forecasts: equal weighting, capitalization weighting, minimum variance, and risk budgeting.

Here **robustness** means how an allocation responds to uncertain inputs and implementation choices. We are not solving a formal worst-case optimization over an uncertainty set. A rule that avoids expected means can still be sensitive to estimated volatilities, correlations, the investment universe, and constraints.

We will answer three questions: how much capital is concentrated, which assets contribute to modeled risk, and how much trading is needed to maintain an allocation. The chapter progresses from small calculations to a monthly industry comparison with explicit estimation timing, drifting holdings, and transaction costs.

Run examples in order. The opening covariance inputs are hypothetical annual quantities; historical returns later in the chapter are monthly decimals. The interactive experiment can also run independently because its helper contains the two-asset inputs.

## Capital Concentration Is Not Risk Diversification

For nonnegative portfolio weights summing to one, the weight Herfindahl index is $H_w=\sum_iw_i^2$. Its reciprocal is the **effective number of holdings**:

$$N_{\rm eff,w}=\frac{1}{\sum_iw_i^2}.$$

An equally weighted $N$-asset portfolio has $N_{\rm eff,w}=N$. An allocation entirely in one asset has a value of one. For $(0.7,0.1,0.1,0.1)$, the value is $1/(0.49+3\times0.01)\approx1.923$: four names, but capital concentration comparable to fewer than two equally weighted positions.

```python:line-numbers
capital_allocations = pd.DataFrame({"Equal weights": [0.25] * 4,
                                    "Concentrated": [0.70, 0.10, 0.10, 0.10]},
                                   index=["A", "B", "C", "D"])
print(capital_allocations)
print("Effective number of holdings:")
print(capital_allocations.apply(pok.enc).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
```
<Editor id="robust-capital-concentration" />

This measure does not use covariance. Four equally weighted investments can still share one dominant economic exposure. The toolkit's `enc()` requires nonnegative weights summing to one; applying the same count interpretation to signed or leveraged positions is inappropriate.

## Attribute Portfolio Volatility with Euler's Theorem

For covariance $\Sigma$ and exposures $\mathbf w$, portfolio volatility is:

$$\sigma_p=\sqrt{\mathbf w^T\Sigma\mathbf w}.$$

Assume positive portfolio variance. Differentiating with respect to one exposure gives its **marginal contribution to volatility**:

$$\operatorname{MRC}_i=\frac{\partial\sigma_p}{\partial w_i}=\frac{(\Sigma\mathbf w)_i}{\sigma_p}.$$

Volatility is homogeneous of degree one for positive exposure scaling. Euler's theorem therefore decomposes it into **component contributions**:

$$\operatorname{RC}_i=w_i\operatorname{MRC}_i,\qquad \sum_i\operatorname{RC}_i=\sigma_p.$$

Dividing by total volatility gives the **relative risk share**:

$$\boxed{q_i=\frac{w_i(\Sigma\mathbf w)_i}{\mathbf w^T\Sigma\mathbf w}},\qquad \sum_iq_i=1.$$

Component volatility has the units of returns; relative risk shares are dimensionless. The corresponding component variance $w_i(\Sigma\mathbf w)_i$ sums to portfolio variance. It is half of $w_i\partial\sigma_p^2/\partial w_i$, because variance is homogeneous of degree two.

Marginal volatility holds the other exposures fixed. A financed reallocation from asset $j$ to $i$ instead has local volatility effect $\operatorname{MRC}_i-\operatorname{MRC}_j$. These are related but different derivatives.

### Worked Example: Equal Capital, Unequal Risk

Let Equity have volatility 20%, Bonds 10%, and correlation 0.25. Their covariance is 0.005. At 50/50 weights:

$$\Sigma=\begin{pmatrix}0.04&0.005\\0.005&0.01\end{pmatrix},\qquad
\Sigma\mathbf w=\begin{pmatrix}0.0225\\0.0075\end{pmatrix}.$$

Portfolio variance is 0.015 and volatility is approximately 12.247%. The component variances are 0.01125 and 0.00375, so Equity supplies **75% of modeled risk** despite receiving only half the capital.

```python:line-numbers
asset_names = pd.Index(["Equity", "Bonds"])
annual_covariance = pd.DataFrame([[0.04, 0.005], [0.005, 0.01]], index=asset_names, columns=asset_names)
equal_weights = pd.Series([0.5, 0.5], index=asset_names)
equal_report = pok.risk_contribution_report(equal_weights, annual_covariance)
equal_volatility = pok.portfolio_volatility(equal_weights, annual_covariance)
print((equal_report * 100).rename(columns={
    "Weight": "Capital (%)", "Marginal volatility": "Marginal vol (percentage points per unit weight)",
    "Component volatility": "Component vol (percentage points)", "Relative risk": "Risk share (%)",
}).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
print(f"Portfolio volatility: {equal_volatility:.3%}")
print(f"Sum of component volatility: {equal_report['Component volatility'].sum():.3%}")
```

The component volatilities are approximately 9.186 and 3.062 percentage points, adding to 12.247%. `portfolio_risk_contributions()` returns the relative shares, while `risk_contribution_report()` exposes all four quantities with asset labels.

### Negative Contributions Can Represent Hedging

Even a long-only position can contribute negatively to portfolio risk when its covariance with the portfolio is negative. That is an attribution result, not a negative standalone variance.

```python:line-numbers
hedge_covariance = pd.DataFrame([[0.04, -0.018], [-0.018, 0.01]], index=asset_names, columns=asset_names)
hedge_weights = pd.Series([0.9, 0.1], index=asset_names)
hedge_report = pok.risk_contribution_report(hedge_weights, hedge_covariance)
print((hedge_report[["Weight", "Relative risk"]] * 100).rename(columns={
    "Weight": "Capital (%)", "Relative risk": "Risk share (%)",
}).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
print(f"Relative contributions sum to: {hedge_report['Relative risk'].sum():.6f}")
```
<Editor id="robust-negative-risk-contribution" />

Here Equity's risk share is about **105.195%** and Bonds' share is **−5.195%**, adding to 100%. A small increase in the bond exposure, holding equity exposure fixed, reduces portfolio volatility. At exactly zero portfolio variance, marginal and relative volatility contributions are undefined; the helper reports that instead of producing misleading divisions by zero.

## Risk-Share Concentration Is Not a Count of Independent Bets

When all relative contributions are nonnegative, their inverse Herfindahl is:

$$N_{\rm eff,q}=\frac{1}{\sum_iq_i^2}.$$

The toolkit retains the historical name `encb()` for this quantity. We interpret it as an **effective number of risk contributors in the chosen asset representation**, not as a count of statistically independent factors. It is unavailable as a count when contributions are signed.

Consider four identical exposures with 20% volatility and perfect correlation. Equal weights give four effective holdings and four equal risk shares, but the covariance matrix has rank one: all four exposures move with the same factor.

```python:line-numbers
one_factor_covariance = pd.DataFrame(0.04, index=list("ABCD"), columns=list("ABCD"))
one_factor_weights = pd.Series(0.25, index=one_factor_covariance.index)
one_factor_shares = pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(one_factor_weights, one_factor_covariance)
print(f"Effective holdings: {pok.enc(one_factor_weights):.1f}")
print(f"Effective risk contributors: {pok.encb(one_factor_shares):.1f}")
print(f"Covariance rank: {np.linalg.matrix_rank(one_factor_covariance)}")
print(f"Portfolio volatility: {pok.portfolio_volatility(one_factor_weights, one_factor_covariance):.1%}")
```
<Editor id="robust-common-factor-counterexample" />

Splitting one exposure into multiple identical labels can improve an asset-level concentration statistic without adding economic diversification. Factor-based diversification requires a specified factor representation, as discussed in the previous chapters.

## Equal Risk Contribution and General Risk Budgets

Choose positive desired risk shares $b_i$, summing to one. A **risk-budget portfolio** satisfies:

$$w_i(\Sigma\mathbf w)_i=b_i\,\mathbf w^T\Sigma\mathbf w.$$

Equal risk contribution (ERC), commonly called asset-level risk parity, uses $b_i=1/N$. It requires a covariance estimate but no explicit expected-return forecast. By contrast, the global minimum-variance (GMV) portfolio minimizes total modeled variance under the chosen constraints. Equalizing contributions and minimizing their total are different objectives.

### When Inverse Volatility Works

For diagonal covariance, $q_i=w_i^2\sigma_i^2/\sigma_p^2$. General positive risk budgets therefore imply:

$$w_i\propto\frac{\sqrt{b_i}}{\sigma_i}.$$

For equal budgets, this reduces to inverse-volatility weighting. In a two-asset portfolio, equalizing the component variances cancels the common covariance term:

$$w_1^2\sigma_1^2=w_2^2\sigma_2^2
\quad\Longrightarrow\quad w_1\sigma_1=w_2\sigma_2.$$

Thus the two-asset ERC weights are inverse volatility for any nondegenerate correlation. At volatilities 20% and 10%, they are **one-third Equity and two-thirds Bonds**.

```python:line-numbers
inverse_volatility_weights = 1 / np.sqrt(np.diag(annual_covariance))
inverse_volatility_weights /= inverse_volatility_weights.sum()
erc_weights = pok.risk_parity_weigths(annual_covariance)
erc_shares = pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(erc_weights, annual_covariance)
print(pd.DataFrame({"Inverse vol weight (%)": inverse_volatility_weights * 100,
                    "ERC weight (%)": erc_weights * 100, "ERC risk share (%)": erc_shares * 100}).to_string())
```
<Editor id="robust-two-asset-risk-parity" />

For three or more assets with unequal correlation patterns, inverse volatility generally does **not** equalize risk. Use volatilities 10%, 20%, and 30% with correlations 0.6, 0.1, and 0.2. Inverse-volatility weights equalize $w_i\sigma_i$, but the correlation-row sums differ, so the relative risk shares differ.

```python:line-numbers
three_assets = pd.Index(["A", "B", "C"])
three_volatilities = np.array([0.10, 0.20, 0.30])
three_correlations = np.array([[1.0, 0.6, 0.1], [0.6, 1.0, 0.2], [0.1, 0.2, 1.0]])
three_covariance = pd.DataFrame(three_correlations * np.outer(three_volatilities, three_volatilities),
                               index=three_assets, columns=three_assets)
three_inverse = pd.Series(1 / three_volatilities, index=three_assets)
three_inverse /= three_inverse.sum()
three_erc = pok.risk_parity_weigths(three_covariance)
print(pd.DataFrame({"Inverse vol weights (%)": three_inverse * 100,
                    "Inverse vol risk shares (%)": pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(three_inverse, three_covariance) * 100,
                    "ERC weights (%)": three_erc * 100,
                    "ERC risk shares (%)": pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(three_erc, three_covariance) * 100})
      .to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
```
<Editor id="robust-inverse-volatility-limit" />

The inverse-volatility risk shares are approximately **35.417%, 37.500%, and 27.083%**; the ERC solution targets one-third each. Equal correlation is another special case in which inverse volatility yields equal risk contributions, but it is a modeling restriction, not a general property of markets.

### A Convex Risk-Budget Construction

For positive-definite covariance and strictly positive budgets, solve for positive unnormalized exposures $\mathbf x$:

$$\min_{\mathbf x>0}\quad\frac12\mathbf x^T\Sigma\mathbf x-\sum_i b_i\log x_i.$$

The first-order conditions give $(\Sigma\mathbf x)_i=b_i/x_i$, hence $x_i(\Sigma\mathbf x)_i=b_i$. Summing gives $\mathbf x^T\Sigma\mathbf x=1$. Normalizing $\mathbf w=\mathbf x/(\mathbf1^T\mathbf x)$ preserves relative risk shares, because both numerator and denominator scale quadratically.

The quadratic term penalizes total risk; the negative-log terms keep every positive-budget asset invested. The intermediate $\mathbf x$ is a computational exposure vector, not a fully invested portfolio. Its unit variance is a consequence of this normalization, not a 100% volatility target for the final weights. The helper solves this problem and then converts $\mathbf x$ into weights summing to one.

The helper checks the achieved budgets after solving. This implementation requires numerically positive-definite covariance; regularize a singular estimate or remove redundant assets before fitting. Zero-budget assets should be excluded from the optimization universe. Negative hedge contributions can be analyzed, but are not targets of this positive risk-budget construction.

```python:line-numbers
desired_budgets = pd.Series([0.20, 0.30, 0.50], index=three_assets)
budget_weights = pd.Series(pok.portfolio_risk_contrib_optimizer(desired_budgets, three_covariance), index=three_assets)
achieved_budgets = pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(budget_weights, three_covariance)
print(pd.DataFrame({"Requested risk (%)": desired_budgets * 100,
                    "Capital weight (%)": budget_weights * 100,
                    "Achieved risk (%)": achieved_budgets * 100}).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.4f}"))
```
<Editor id="robust-positive-risk-budgets" />

**Try it:** change the desired budgets to equal thirds. Then increase Asset C's budget while keeping all budgets positive and their sum equal to one. More capital is not necessarily required in proportion to the risk budget: volatility and correlation also enter the solution.

## Historical Comparison on One Investment Universe

### Data, Estimation Windows, and Baselines

Use the 30 value-weighted industry portfolios from January 1990 through December 2018. Estimate covariance from the previous **60 months**, then allocate for the next month. All strategies are long-only, fully invested, and evaluated over the same **288 months: January 1995–December 2018**.

The strategies are EW, lagged capitalization weighting (CW), sample-covariance GMV, sample-covariance ERC, and ERC with 50% constant-correlation shrinkage. This is allocation **across industries**, not equal weighting of the individual stocks within each industry.

The shrinkage estimate puts equal weight on the sample covariance and the constant-correlation target from [Chapter 2.2](./2.2-covariance.md). The target retains the sample variances and replaces pairwise correlations with their average.

```python:line-numbers

print(f"Evaluation: {evaluation_dates[0]} to {evaluation_dates[-1]}, {len(evaluation_dates)} months")
```
<Editor id="robust-industry-data" />

Capitalization fractions come from firm counts times average firm sizes. CW uses the final training month's weights, not the evaluated month's weights. The historical files do not establish when all metadata were published, so this is a retrospective comparison rather than a fully point-in-time investability claim.

### Retain the Allocation History

Keep target weights as well as returns so that we can examine concentration, drift, and turnover. Each training slice ends just before the month in which its allocation is used. No expected-return estimate enters GMV or ERC.

## How Sensitive Are the Allocations to the Sample?

Resample complete monthly return rows from the first training window, preserving contemporaneous cross-asset relationships. Re-estimate the same 50% shrinkage covariance for both GMV and ERC. This isolates how the two allocation rules respond to one form of estimation uncertainty.

The illustrative bootstrap treats months as independent and identically distributed (iid). It does not preserve serial dependence or establish confidence intervals for a changing future regime. Each repetition gives GMV and ERC the same resampled rows; the fixed seed makes the experiment reproducible.

```python:line-numbers
first_training = industry_returns.iloc[:window_months]
bootstrap_rng = np.random.default_rng(42)
bootstrap_allocations = {"GMV": [], "ERC": []}
for repetition in range(40):
    sampled_rows = bootstrap_rng.integers(0, window_months, size=window_months)
    resampled_returns = first_training.iloc[sampled_rows].reset_index(drop=True)
    bootstrap_allocations["GMV"].append(pok.weight_minvar(resampled_returns, cov_estimator=pok.shrinkage_cov, delta=0.5))
    bootstrap_allocations["ERC"].append(pok.weight_rp(resampled_returns, cov_estimator=pok.shrinkage_cov, delta=0.5))
allocation_sensitivity = pd.DataFrame({name: pd.DataFrame(rows).std(ddof=1) * 100 for name, rows in bootstrap_allocations.items()})
print("Weight standard deviation across resamples (percentage points):")
print(allocation_sensitivity.to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
sensitive_industries = allocation_sensitivity.max(axis=1).nlargest(8).index
print("\n<ECHARTS_DATA>" + json.dumps({
    "allocationSensitivity": {
        "type": "bar", "title": "Sampling Sensitivity: Same Resamples and Covariance Estimator",
        "xAxis": {"type": "category", "data": sensitive_industries.tolist()},
        "yAxisName": "Weight standard deviation (percentage points)",
        "series": {name: allocation_sensitivity.loc[sensitive_industries, name].tolist() for name in allocation_sensitivity},
    },
}, allow_nan=False))
```
<Editor id="robust-allocation-sensitivity" />

The chart selects the eight industries with the largest weight standard deviation under either method. A value of 2 means **2 percentage points** of weight variability across the 40 resamples, not 2% of the average weight. These are in-sample allocation sensitivities, not realized return volatilities.

Weight stability is useful but not sufficient: a stable rule can consistently target the wrong risk structure. Interpret this experiment alongside its covariance assumptions, the historical evaluation, and economic exposure analysis rather than declaring one rule universally robust.

## Turnover and Transaction Costs

### Drift Before the Next Rebalance

Let $w_{t-1,i}$ be the previous beginning-period target and $r_{t-1,i}$ the subsequent asset return. Before the next rebalance, the actual weight is:

$$d_{t,i}=\frac{w_{t-1,i}(1+r_{t-1,i})}{1+\mathbf w_{t-1}^T\mathbf r_{t-1}}.$$

Compare the new target with these **drifted weights**, not just with the preceding target. One-way turnover, before accounting for fee financing, is:

$$T_t=\frac12\sum_i|w_{t,i}-d_{t,i}|.$$

Even unchanged equal-weight targets require trading after unequal asset returns. Suppose a 50/50 portfolio earns 10% on A and −10% on B. Portfolio wealth is unchanged, but weights drift to 55%/45%; restoring 50/50 requires 5% one-way turnover.

```python:line-numbers
previous_target = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
realized_asset_returns = np.array([0.10, -0.10])
drifted_weights = previous_target * (1 + realized_asset_returns) / (1 + previous_target @ realized_asset_returns)
next_target = np.array([0.5, 0.5])
worked_turnover = np.abs(next_target - drifted_weights).sum() / 2
print("Drifted weights:", drifted_weights)
print(f"One-way turnover despite unchanged target: {worked_turnover:.1%}")
```
<Editor id="robust-turnover-worked-example" />

For the historical comparison, assume each account is already invested in its first target at the start of evaluation. We exclude initial funding costs equally across strategies, and charge subsequent rebalancing costs.

```python:line-numbers
pretrade_weights = {}
turnover = pd.DataFrame(index=evaluation_dates)
for name, targets in weight_history.items():
    drifted = targets.shift(1) * (1 + evaluation_assets.shift(1))
    drifted = drifted.div(1 + gross_returns[name].shift(1), axis=0)
    drifted.iloc[0] = targets.iloc[0]
    pretrade_weights[name] = drifted
    turnover[name] = (targets - drifted).abs().sum(axis=1) / 2
print("Mean monthly one-way turnover (%):")
```

### Pay Fees from the Portfolio

Suppose the cost rate is $k$ per dollar **bought or sold**. If $V$ is wealth before trading and $aV$ remains afterward, the dollar trade in asset $i$ is $V(aw_i-d_i)$. Self-financing therefore requires:

$$a=1-k\sum_i|aw_i-d_i|.$$

This accounts for the sale of assets needed to pay fees. For long-only fully invested weights and $0\leq k<1$, the right side is a contraction in $a$, so fixed-point iteration converges. Once the targets are established, the period's net return is:

$$R_{p,t}^{\rm net}=a_t(1+R_{p,t}^{\rm gross})-1.$$

The usual approximation $a\approx1-2kT$ can be close at small costs, but omits the fee-financing adjustment. The factor of two matters: one-way turnover is half the total purchases and sales.

Start with $a=1$, calculate the required trades, deduct their fees, and repeat until the remaining-wealth fraction stops changing. We check convergence rather than assume a fixed number of iterations is sufficient. Fees reduce wealth at the start of the month; the remaining capital then earns that month's asset returns. Because post-trade holdings have the same target proportions, the drift calculation above also applies to the net portfolio.

```python:line-numbers
cost_bps = 10.0
fee_rate = cost_bps / 10000
if not np.isfinite(fee_rate) or not 0 <= fee_rate < 1:
    raise ValueError("Use a finite cost rate between 0 and 10,000 basis points, excluding 10,000")
after_cost_fractions = pd.DataFrame(index=evaluation_dates)
net_returns = pd.DataFrame(index=evaluation_dates)
for name, targets in weight_history.items():
    remaining_fraction = pd.Series(1.0, index=evaluation_dates)
    for iteration in range(100):
        traded_fraction = targets.mul(remaining_fraction, axis=0).sub(pretrade_weights[name]).abs().sum(axis=1)
        updated_fraction = 1 - fee_rate * traded_fraction
        difference = (updated_fraction - remaining_fraction).abs().max()
        remaining_fraction = updated_fraction
        if difference <= 1e-12:
            break
    else:
        raise RuntimeError("Fee calculation did not converge; increase the iteration limit")
    after_cost_fractions[name] = remaining_fraction
    net_returns[name] = remaining_fraction * (1 + gross_returns[name]) - 1
cost_comparison = pd.DataFrame({
    "Gross CAGR (%)": pok.annualize_rets(gross_returns, 12) * 100,
    "Net CAGR (%)": pok.annualize_rets(net_returns, 12) * 100,
    "Mean monthly turnover (%)": turnover.mean() * 100,
})
print(f"Illustrative cost: {cost_bps:g} bp per dollar bought or sold; initial funding cost excluded")
print(cost_comparison.to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
net_wealth = 100 * (1 + net_returns).cumprod()
net_wealth.loc[evaluation_dates[0] - 1] = 100.0
net_wealth = net_wealth.sort_index()

    },
```

## Scaling Risk Parity Is a Separate Financing Decision

An unlevered risk-budget portfolio can have low volatility because it puts more capital in low-volatility assets. To reach a chosen modeled risk level, investors sometimes scale the risky portfolio and borrow or lend cash. With risky fraction $a\geq0$ and a known cash return $r_f$:

$$R_c=r_f+a(R_p-r_f),\qquad \sigma_c=a\sigma_p.$$

Positive scaling preserves relative risky-asset contributions, but $a>1$ requires borrowing. A target based on estimated covariance is not a guarantee of realized future volatility, and borrowing spreads and constraints matter.

```python:line-numbers
requested_volatility = 0.20
erc_volatility = pok.portfolio_volatility(erc_weights, annual_covariance)
risky_fraction = requested_volatility / erc_volatility
scaled_exposures = risky_fraction * erc_weights
cash_weight = 1 - risky_fraction
print(f"Unlevered ERC volatility: {erc_volatility:.3%}")
print(f"Risky exposure relative to account net asset value: {risky_fraction:.3f}")
print("Scaled risky exposures (% of account net asset value):")
print((scaled_exposures * 100).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
print(f"Cash allocation: {cash_weight:.3%}")
print("Relative risky-asset contributions:")
print(pok.portfolio_risk_contributions(scaled_exposures, annual_covariance).to_string())
```

For the hypothetical inputs, ERC volatility is about 10.541%, so a 20% modeled target requires roughly **1.897 times risky exposure**, financed with about **89.7% cash borrowing relative to account net asset value**. For every 100 units of the investor's capital, this means approximately 63.25 in Equity, 126.49 in Bonds, and −89.74 in cash. The historical strategy comparison above uses no such leverage. Mixing levered and unlevered strategies without accounting for financing would answer a different question.

## Chapter Summary

- Capital concentration, risk-share concentration, and economic diversification are distinct. Many names do not necessarily mean many independent sources of risk.
- Euler attribution separates marginal volatility, component volatility, and relative risk shares. Hedge contributions can be negative; zero-risk portfolios require separate treatment.
- Inverse volatility is an ERC solution in special cases, not a general substitute for the covariance-aware calculation.
- Positive risk budgets can be solved through a convex formulation and must be checked after numerical optimization. They describe the supplied risk model, not guaranteed realized contributions.
- Compare allocation methods on common assets, constraints, information dates, and evaluation periods. Examine sampling sensitivity as well as realized outcomes.
- Drift, turnover, self-financed costs, and any leverage are part of the investment problem. A strategy that looks attractive before those effects may be less attractive to implement.

## Analysis of Factor Investing

A **factor** summarizes a common source of return variation. An **exposure**, or loading, measures how a portfolio's return responds to that factor in a specified model. A **factor premium** is an expected reward for exposure, not the factor's realized return in every period. Neither a positive historical average nor an economic risk story guarantees a positive future premium.

### Categories of Factors

- **Macroeconomic factors** describe economic conditions or innovations: inflation surprises, growth shocks, or changes in interest rates. An oil-price change can benefit producers and hurt consumers, so exposures can have opposite signs.
- **Statistical factors** summarize shared variation extracted from data, for example principal components. Their economic interpretation is a separate question.
- **Style factors** are associated with characteristics such as market capitalization, book-to-market, momentum, or profitability. They are often represented by the return spread between portfolios sorted on those characteristics.

A macroeconomic measurement need not be a traded return. When we use a tradable factor portfolio, its return has a direct investment interpretation; otherwise, the units of the exposure depend on the units of the explanatory variable.

### Factor Models

Let $R_{i,t}$ be a portfolio's simple return and $R_{f,t}$ the matched cash return. A time-series factor model is:

$$
R_{i,t}-R_{f,t}
=\alpha_i+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{i,k}F_{k,t}+\varepsilon_{i,t}.
$$

Here $F_{k,t}$ is a **realized factor observation**, $\beta_{i,k}$ its loading, $\alpha_i$ an intercept, and $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ the unexplained return. All observations must refer to the same period. For return factors expressed as decimals, the loadings are dimensionless and alpha is a return **per observation period**.

Under the model's zero-mean residual condition, taking expectations gives:

$$
\mathbb{E}[R_i-R_f]
=\alpha_i+\sum_{k=1}^{K}\beta_{i,k}\mathbb{E}[F_k].
$$

This separates realized attribution from expected premiums. A fitted model is descriptive; using it to forecast requires assumptions about future exposures and factor expectations. An equilibrium pricing model imposes additional restrictions, such as zero alpha, which an arbitrary regression does not establish.

## The Capital Asset Pricing Model (CAPM)

CAPM is a single-period equilibrium model with a common risk-free opportunity, mean–variance investors, homogeneous beliefs, and frictionless trading under its standard assumptions. It prices exposure to the aggregate market portfolio. Its **Security Market Line (SML)** is:

$$
\mathbb{E}[R_i]=r_f+\beta_i\bigl(\mathbb{E}[R_M]-r_f\bigr),
\qquad
\beta_i=\frac{\operatorname{Cov}(R_i,R_M)}{\operatorname{Var}(R_M)}.
$$

The risk-free return $r_f$ is known for this investment horizon, and market variance is positive. The SML relates expected return to **beta**. It differs from the capital allocation lines in Part 1, which relate expected return to **total volatility** for mixtures of cash and a risky fund.

### Beta Is Not Correlation

For positive volatilities:

$$
\beta_i=\rho_{i,M}\frac{\sigma_i}{\sigma_M}.
$$

Correlation measures standardized co-movement; beta also reflects the asset's volatility relative to the market. An asset with correlation 0.6 and twice market volatility has beta 1.2. An asset with correlation 0.3 and four times market volatility has the **same beta**. Their idiosyncratic risk can be very different.

**Hand check.** If the one-year cash return is 2%, expected market return is 8%, and beta is 1.2, CAPM implies $2\%+1.2(8\%-2\%)=9.2\%$. This is a model-implied expected return, not a promised outcome.

```python:line-numbers
cash_return, expected_market_return = 0.02, 0.08
market_volatility = 0.15
asset_volatilities = np.array([0.30, 0.60])
correlations = np.array([0.60, 0.30])
betas = correlations * asset_volatilities / market_volatility
capm_expected_returns = cash_return + betas * (expected_market_return - cash_return)
print(pd.DataFrame({
    "Correlation": correlations,
    "Beta": betas,
    "CAPM expected return (%)": capm_expected_returns * 100,
}, index=["Asset A", "Asset B"]).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
```

### Estimating a Market Regression

For historical observations, define $y_t=R_{i,t}-R_{f,t}$ and $x_t=R_{M,t}-R_{f,t}$. Estimate:

$$
y_t=\alpha+\beta x_t+\varepsilon_t,
\qquad
\min_{\alpha,\beta}\sum_t(y_t-\alpha-\beta x_t)^2.
$$

The first-order conditions set the residual sum and its cross-product with $x$ to zero. Subtracting the sample means gives:

$$
\hat\beta=\frac{\sum_t(x_t-\bar x)(y_t-\bar y)}{\sum_t(x_t-\bar x)^2},
\qquad
\hat\alpha=\bar y-\hat\beta\bar x.
$$

When cash varies across historical months, use the **matched excess-return series** in this formula. Beta estimated from raw returns need not be identical.

Consider four monthly market excess returns: −3%, −1%, 1%, and 3%. Construct asset excess returns as $0.1\%+1.2x_t+\varepsilon_t$, with residuals 0.2%, −0.2%, −0.2%, and 0.2%. The residuals sum to zero and have zero cross-product with $x$, so OLS must recover alpha 0.1% and beta 1.2 exactly.

```python:line-numbers
market_excess = pd.Series([-0.03, -0.01, 0.01, 0.03], name="Market")
residuals = np.array([0.002, -0.002, -0.002, 0.002])
asset_excess = 0.001 + 1.2 * market_excess + residuals
beta_by_hand = market_excess.cov(asset_excess) / market_excess.var()
alpha_by_hand = asset_excess.mean() - beta_by_hand * market_excess.mean()
worked_fit = pok.linear_regression(asset_excess, market_excess.to_frame())
print(f"Hand calculation: beta {beta_by_hand:.4f}; monthly alpha {alpha_by_hand:.2%}")
print(worked_fit.params.to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.4f}"))
print(f"Residual sum: {worked_fit.resid.sum():.2e}")
```

Four artificial observations verify the algebra, not statistical reliability. In a real sample, alpha, beta, and their uncertainty depend on the sample window, explanatory factors, and assumptions about the errors.

## Value, Growth, and the Fama–French Factors

### Price-to-Book and Book-to-Market

For a firm with positive common book equity, market-to-book compares the market value of common equity with its accounting book value:

$$
P/B=\frac{\text{market capitalization}}{\text{common book equity}},
\qquad B/P=\frac{1}{P/B}.
$$

Book equity is an accounting residual after liabilities and relevant non-common-equity claims; it is not the book value of one asset. For market capitalization of \$200 million and common book equity of \$100 million, $P/B=2$ and $B/P=0.5$.

**Value** stocks have relatively high book-to-market ratios; **growth** stocks have relatively low ratios within a stated sorting universe. These classifications do not prove undervaluation or overvaluation. Differences can reflect growth expectations, profitability, distress, accounting practices, or intangible assets. There is no universal 15% ROE threshold defining growth stocks, and negative book equity requires separate treatment.

### Fama–French Three-Factor Model

The three-factor time-series specification adds two long–short return spreads to the market excess return:

$$
R_{i,t}-R_{f,t}=\alpha_i
+\beta_{i,M}(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})
+\beta_{i,S}\operatorname{SMB}_t
+\beta_{i,H}\operatorname{HML}_t+\varepsilon_{i,t}.
$$

In the standard US construction, stocks are assigned to **six value-weighted portfolios** from two size groups (Small, Big) and three book-to-market groups (Low, Neutral, High). The sorts use NYSE-based breakpoints; these factors are not simply bottom-decile minus top-decile returns. With $R_{S,H}$ denoting the small/high portfolio return:

$$
\operatorname{SMB}
=\frac{R_{S,L}+R_{S,N}+R_{S,H}}{3}
-\frac{R_{B,L}+R_{B,N}+R_{B,H}}{3},
$$

$$
\operatorname{HML}
=\frac{R_{S,H}+R_{B,H}}{2}
-\frac{R_{S,L}+R_{B,L}}{2}.
$$

SMB means **Small Minus Big**; HML means **High Minus Low** book-to-market. Both are return spreads whose component weights sum to zero. Do not subtract cash again from these supplied spreads or from the supplied `Mkt-RF` column. Cash is subtracted from the dependent asset's total return.

**Hand check.** Suppose the six portfolios return 1%, 2%, 4% for Small and 0%, 1%, 3% for Big in one month. SMB is 1%; HML is 3%. For market excess return 2%, loadings $(0.8,-0.2,0.4)$, and cash return 0.1%, the zero-alpha factor benchmark return is $0.1\%+0.8(2\%)-0.2(1\%)+0.4(3\%)=2.7\%$.

```python:line-numbers
small = np.array([0.01, 0.02, 0.04])
big = np.array([0.00, 0.01, 0.03])
smb = small.mean() - big.mean()
hml = (small[2] + big[2] - small[0] - big[0]) / 2
factor_benchmark = 0.001 + 0.8 * 0.02 - 0.2 * smb + 0.4 * hml
print(f"SMB: {smb:.2%}; HML: {hml:.2%}")
print(f"One-month factor benchmark: {factor_benchmark:.2%}")
```
<Editor id="fama-french-construction-example" />

The Carhart four-factor model adds a momentum spread. A common construction ranks past performance over months 2 through 12 before the holding month, omitting the most recent month. The exact sorting, weighting, and rebalancing rules must accompany a chosen dataset. Momentum is not a guarantee that recent winners continue winning; we do not estimate that fourth factor from this three-factor file.

Factor definitions and formation rules are documented in the [Kenneth French Data Library](https://mba.tuck.dartmouth.edu/pages/faculty/ken.french/data_library.html). Our bundled CSV is a fixed historical snapshot, not a live update.

## Factor Benchmarks and the Meaning of Alpha

For the market model, the zero-alpha benchmark return is:

$$
R_{B,t}=R_{f,t}+\beta(R_{M,t}-R_{f,t})
=\beta R_{M,t}+(1-\beta)R_{f,t}.
$$

For each dollar of equity, beta 1.3 means \$1.30 of market exposure financed with \$0.30 cash borrowing. Beta 0.55 means \$0.55 in the market and \$0.45 in cash. This interpretation assumes the stated financing rate and feasible positions. Multifactor long–short spreads add zero-net-investment exposures; their loadings are not ordinary long-only allocation weights.

With a constant exposure estimated in-sample, OLS with an intercept makes the **sample mean** of asset return minus benchmark return equal to fitted alpha. It does not make every month's difference equal to alpha. An estimated positive alpha can reflect skill, omitted risks, chance, or measurement choices; it is not proof of manager value added. Berkshire is also a company return, not a fee-adjusted mutual-fund mandate.

Monthly alpha multiplied by 12 is an **annualized arithmetic intercept**. Compounding it as $(1+\alpha)^{12}-1$ would describe a hypothetical constant return process, not the observed annual excess performance of the asset over its benchmark.

## Factor Analysis of Warren Buffett's Berkshire Hathaway

### Load and Align the Historical Observations

The bundled Berkshire file contains daily decimal returns from January 1990 through December 2018. Compound the observed daily returns within each calendar month:

$$R_{i,m}=\prod_{d\in m}(1+R_{i,d})-1.$$

The factor CSV contains monthly percent observations from July 1926 through December 2018. Its loader divides by 100 and creates a monthly `PeriodIndex`. The Berkshire loader creates the same index when aggregating. It rejects missing observations and empty calendar months; neither loader reconstructs missing trading days or establishes the original vendor's adjustment conventions from the CSV alone.


### CAPM Regression and Statistical Uncertainty

Fit the market regression to monthly excess returns. The helper adds an `Alpha` intercept and preserves coefficient names. Default OLS standard errors assume homoskedastic, serially uncorrelated errors. Below we also report **heteroskedasticity and autocorrelation consistent (HAC)** standard errors with six monthly lags, a finite-sample correction, and asymptotic normal reference inference. HAC changes estimated uncertainty, not the fitted coefficients; the lag choice is an assumption to examine.

```python:line-numbers
capm_fit = pok.linear_regression(brka_excess, factor_sample[["Mkt-RF"]])
hac_lags = 6
capm_hac = capm_fit.get_robustcov_results(cov_type="HAC", maxlags=hac_lags,
                                       use_correction=True, use_t=False)
capm_table = pd.DataFrame({
    "Coefficient": capm_fit.params,
    "OLS standard error": capm_fit.bse,
    "HAC standard error": capm_hac.bse,
    "HAC p-value": capm_hac.pvalues,
})
print(capm_table.to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.5f}"))
print(f"Monthly alpha: {capm_fit.params['Alpha']:.3%}")
print(f"12 × monthly alpha: {12 * capm_fit.params['Alpha']:.3%}")
print(f"In-sample R-squared: {capm_fit.rsquared:.3f}")
```
<Editor id="berkshire-capm-regression" />

Interpret beta as sensitivity per unit of market excess return. Interpret alpha in monthly return units. The p-value concerns the zero-coefficient null under the specified inference assumptions; it is not the probability that a manager has skill. A confidence interval and the size of the estimated return are more informative than a significance label alone.

For this snapshot and window, market beta is approximately **0.555**, monthly alpha **0.634%**, and $R^2$ **0.163**. The market-only benchmark therefore combines about 55.5% market exposure with 44.5% cash. Its modest explanatory power leaves substantial monthly variation outside the market factor.

**Try it:** change `hac_lags` to 3 or 12 and rerun this cell. The coefficient estimates stay fixed while their estimated uncertainty changes.

Use the **same dependent returns and months** for the three-factor model. A negative SMB loading is a large-company tilt relative to this factor definition; a positive HML loading is a value tilt. ### Reconstruct the Benchmark and Attribute the Mean

For the fitted three-factor exposures, construct a zero-alpha benchmark from cash plus factor returns. Keep the distinction between **fitted return**, which includes the intercept, and **factor benchmark return**, which excludes it:

$$
\widehat R_{i,t}=R_{B,t}+\hat\alpha,
\qquad R_{i,t}-R_{B,t}=\hat\alpha+\hat\varepsilon_t.
$$

```python:line-numbers
factor_contributions = factor_sample[factor_names].mul(ff_fit.params[factor_names], axis=1)
factor_benchmark_returns = factor_sample["RF"] + factor_contributions.sum(axis=1)
active_returns = factor_sample["BRKA"] - factor_benchmark_returns
mean_attribution = factor_contributions.mean()
mean_attribution["Cash"] = factor_sample["RF"].mean()
mean_attribution["Alpha"] = ff_fit.params["Alpha"]
print("Arithmetic mean monthly contributions (%):")
print((mean_attribution * 100).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.4f}"))
print(f"Sum of contributions: {mean_attribution.sum():.4%}")
print(f"Observed Berkshire mean: {factor_sample['BRKA'].mean():.4%}")
print(f"Mean return minus benchmark: {active_returns.mean():.4%}")
factor_wealth = 100 * (1 + pd.DataFrame({
    "Berkshire": factor_sample["BRKA"], "Zero-alpha benchmark": factor_benchmark_returns,
})).cumprod()

    "dates": factor_wealth.index.astype(str).tolist(),

        "title": "In-Sample Factor Benchmark: Growth of 100",
        "yAxis": {"type": "log", "name": "Wealth per 100 initial units"},
        "series": {name: factor_wealth[name].tolist() for name in factor_wealth},
```

## Sharpe Returns-Based Style Analysis

Factor regression permits signed loadings and an intercept. **Returns-based style analysis** instead asks which mix of chosen benchmark indices best replicates a manager's returns under allocation constraints. For building-block returns $B_{t,j}$, we use a long-only, fully invested portfolio:

$$
\min_{\mathbf w}\frac1T\sum_{t=1}^{T}
\left(R_{m,t}-\sum_{j=1}^{N}w_j B_{t,j}\right)^2,
\qquad w_j\geq0,\quad\sum_jw_j=1.
$$

This chapter's implementation has **no intercept**. We choose to minimize the squared level of the active return, penalizing both mean difference and variability. A specification with a freely fitted intercept would instead absorb the mean difference and minimize demeaned residual variability. Those are different objectives and can give different weights.

### Fit Distance Versus Tracking-Error Volatility

Let $a_t=R_{m,t}-\mathbf w^T\mathbf B_t$. Three quantities must be distinguished:

$$
\text{fit distance}=\sqrt{\sum_t a_t^2},\qquad
\operatorname{RMSE}=\sqrt{\frac1T\sum_t a_t^2},\qquad
\operatorname{TE}_{\rm monthly}=\sqrt{\frac{\sum_t(a_t-\bar a)^2}{T-1}}.
$$

The toolkit's historical name `tracking_error()` denotes the first quantity, retained for compatibility. Conventional tracking-error **volatility** is the third. Their relationship is:

$$\operatorname{RMSE}^2=\bar a^2+\frac{T-1}{T}\operatorname{TE}_{\rm monthly}^2.$$

For four active returns all equal to 1%, fit distance is 2%, RMSE is 1%, and tracking-error volatility is zero. A stable performance shortfall can therefore have zero tracking-error volatility.

```python:line-numbers
constant_active = pd.Series([0.01] * 4)
fit_distance = pok.tracking_error(constant_active, constant_active * 0)
rmse = np.sqrt((constant_active ** 2).mean())
tracking_volatility = constant_active.std(ddof=1)
print(f"Fit distance: {fit_distance:.2%}; RMSE: {rmse:.2%}; tracking volatility: {tracking_volatility:.2%}")
```

### Industry-Based Replication and Unconstrained Regression

Now use the 30 value-weighted industry portfolios from 2000 through 2018. Build a synthetic manager with 30% Beer, 50% Smoke, and 20% Finance exposure, plus a small seeded residual. The residual is a modeling disturbance, **not an investable fourth allocation**. The known benchmark weights already sum to one.

### Berkshire: In-Sample Style Versus Held-Out Replication

Apply the same constrained model to Berkshire, recognizing that an industry mix omits its cash, insurance liabilities, financing, private operations, and changing holdings. Fit weights on **2000–2008**, freeze them, and evaluate **2009–2018**. Separately refit on the later period to see how hindsight changes the result.

```python:line-numbers
style_sample = pd.concat([brka_monthly.rename("BRKA"), industry_vw], axis=1, join="inner").loc["2000":"2018"]
training = style_sample.loc[:"2008"]
held_out = style_sample.loc["2009":]

    ("Held-out frozen weights", held_out, training_weights),
    ("Held-out hindsight refit", held_out, hindsight_weights),
]:

    style_diagnostics[name] = [active.mean() * 100, np.sqrt((active ** 2).mean()) * 100,
                               active.std(ddof=1) * np.sqrt(12) * 100]

print((training_weights.nlargest(8) * 100).to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.2f}"))
```

### Style Drift: Rolling Exposures

A rolling estimate fits only the most recent window. Apparent style drift can reflect actual changes in exposure, sampling noise, correlated benchmarks, or omitted risks. Short windows adapt faster but generally produce noisier estimates; long windows smooth changes and can mix different regimes.

Use a controlled experiment with two industries. The manager's Beer weight changes from 80% to 20% in January 2009, with the rest in Smoke. For each month $t$, estimate weights using the **previous 60 months**, then apply them to month $t$. The chart labels the month in which the estimate is used, not the final month of its training window.

```python:line-numbers
drift_blocks = industry_sample[["Beer", "Smoke"]]
actual_beer_weight = pd.Series(np.where(drift_blocks.index < pd.Period("2009-01", freq="M"), 0.8, 0.2), index=drift_blocks.index)
drift_manager = actual_beer_weight * drift_blocks["Beer"] + (1 - actual_beer_weight) * drift_blocks["Smoke"]
window_months = 60
rolling_rows = []
for t in range(window_months, len(drift_blocks)):
    rolling_rows.append(pok.style_analysis(drift_manager.iloc[t-window_months:t],
                                          drift_blocks.iloc[t-window_months:t]))
rolling_styles = pd.DataFrame(rolling_rows, index=drift_blocks.index[window_months:])
rolling_replication = (rolling_styles * drift_blocks.loc[rolling_styles.index]).sum(axis=1)
print(f"Forecast months: {rolling_styles.index[0]} to {rolling_styles.index[-1]}")
print(f"One-month-ahead replication RMSE: {np.sqrt(((drift_manager.loc[rolling_styles.index] - rolling_replication) ** 2).mean()):.3%}")
print("\n<ECHARTS_DATA>" + json.dumps({
    "dates": rolling_styles.index.astype(str).tolist(),

        "title": "Rolling Style: Known Change and Lagged Estimate",
        "yAxisName": "Beer portfolio weight (%)",
        "series": {"Actual": (actual_beer_weight.loc[rolling_styles.index] * 100).tolist(),
                   "Prior-window estimate": (rolling_styles["Beer"] * 100).tolist()},
        "seriesOptions": {"Actual": {"step": "start"}},
    },
}, allow_nan=False))
```

## Weighting Schemes as Sources of Style Exposure

### Equally Weighted Versus Value-Weighted Industry Portfolios

“Value-weighted” here means **market-capitalization-weighted**, not value-stock-weighted. Equal weighting within an industry generally gives smaller firms more weight relative to capitalization weighting. The return difference can therefore reflect size exposure, rebalancing, and implementation costs, rather than unexplained skill.

Compare the bundled EW and VW versions of each of the 30 industries over 1945–2018. Use the observed monthly `RF` series for both. Compute each industry's conventional annualized arithmetic Sharpe from its monthly excess returns:

$$\widehat{\mathrm{SR}}_{\rm ann}=\sqrt{12}\,\frac{\overline{R-R_f}}{s(R-R_f)}.$$


### Equal Weighting with Capitalization Limits

A portfolio can limit small-component exposure by requiring $w_i\leq m c_i$, where $c_i$ is its capitalization fraction and $m$ is a chosen multiple. If an eligibility threshold excludes a component, its upper bound becomes zero. Full investment is feasible only if the remaining upper bounds sum to at least one.

Clipping equal weights and then renormalizing is not sufficient: renormalization can lift capped weights back above their limits. Instead, retain equal allocations among unconstrained components and redistribute only to components with remaining capacity. This produces:

$$w_i=\min(\lambda,u_i),\qquad \sum_iw_i=1,$$

where $u_i$ is the eligible upper bound and $\lambda$ is chosen to satisfy the budget.

**Hand check.** For cap weights $(0.05,0.15,0.80)$ and $m=2$, the limits are $(0.10,0.30,1.60)$. Capped equal weighting gives $(0.10,0.30,0.60)$. Clipping one-third weights then renormalizing would violate the first two caps.

```python:line-numbers
cap_example = pd.DataFrame([[0.05, 0.15, 0.80]], columns=["Small", "Medium", "Large"],
```

### A Lagged Industry Allocation Comparison

Use the 49-industry return and capitalization metadata. Estimate each month's industry capitalization as firm count times average firm size, then normalize across industries. For return month $t$, the weighting rule uses capitalization at **$t-1$**. This is why the return input includes December 1973 before the evaluation starts in January 1974.

These EW and cap-weight rules do not estimate a covariance matrix, so a one-row training window is sufficient. A 52-row window on monthly data would mean 52 months, not a year—and using its first capitalization row would introduce a stale allocation rather than a current lagged one.

```python:line-numbers
industry49 = pok.get_ind_file(filetype="rets", nind=49, ew=False).loc["1973-12":"2018"]
firm_counts49 = pok.get_ind_file(filetype="nfirms", nind=49).loc[industry49.index]
firm_sizes49 = pok.get_ind_file(filetype="size", nind=49).loc[industry49.index]
capitalization49 = firm_counts49 * firm_sizes49
cap49 = capitalization49.div(capitalization49.sum(axis=1), axis=0)
allocation_returns = pd.DataFrame({
    "Equal industry weights": pok.backtest_weight_scheme(industry49, window=1, weight_scheme=pok.weight_ew),
    "Lagged capitalization": pok.backtest_weight_scheme(industry49, window=1, weight_scheme=pok.weight_cw, cap_ws=cap49),
    "Capped equal weights": pok.backtest_weight_scheme(industry49, window=1, weight_scheme=pok.weight_ew,
                                                       cap_ws=cap49, max_cw_mult=5, microcap_thr=0.005),
}).iloc[1:]
allocation_excess = allocation_returns.sub(factors_monthly.loc[allocation_returns.index, "RF"], axis=0)
allocation_statistics = pd.DataFrame({
    "CAGR (%)": pok.annualize_rets(allocation_returns, 12) * 100,
    "Annualized volatility (%)": pok.annualize_vol(allocation_returns, 12) * 100,
    "Sharpe": np.sqrt(12) * allocation_excess.mean() / allocation_excess.std(ddof=1),
})
print(f"Evaluation: {allocation_returns.index[0]} to {allocation_returns.index[-1]}, {len(allocation_returns)} months")
print(allocation_statistics.to_string(float_format=lambda x: f"{x:.3f}"))
allocation_wealth = 100 * (1 + allocation_returns).cumprod()
allocation_wealth.loc[allocation_returns.index[0] - 1] = 100.0

    },
```

The legacy parameter name `microcap_thr` applies here to an **industry's share of aggregate capitalization**, not to the size of individual stocks. The 0.5% threshold excludes small industries; the five-times cap limits remaining industry allocations. All three strategies rebalance monthly and omit trading costs. Lagging avoids using the return month's capitalization, but these files do not establish when the metadata were published or how historical constituents were revised.

## Chapter Summary

- Factor loadings measure modeled sensitivity; expected premiums, realized factor returns, and portfolio weights are distinct quantities.
- CAPM beta is covariance divided by market variance, not correlation. The SML uses beta rather than total volatility.
- Monthly factor regressions require aligned dates, consistent units, and matched cash subtraction. Alpha is a model- and sample-dependent intercept.
- Constrained style replication can produce an investable benchmark mix, but a good return fit does not identify actual holdings or establish skill.
- Frozen-sample and rolling evaluations must respect the information available before each evaluated return.
- Equal weighting, capitalization weighting, and exposure caps change style as well as performance. Benchmark design and implementation assumptions belong in the interpretation.

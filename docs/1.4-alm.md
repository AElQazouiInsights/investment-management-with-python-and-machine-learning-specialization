# {{ $frontmatter.title }}

The preceding chapter asked how to protect portfolio wealth. **Asset–liability management (ALM)** asks whether that wealth can meet specified obligations **when they fall due**. A pension payment, tuition bill, or debt repayment gives the investment objective an amount, date, and currency; a high portfolio return alone does not establish success.

The chapter develops that question in stages:

| Investment question | Main idea |
| --- | --- |
| What are future obligations worth today? | Discount factors and present value. |
| Are today's assets sufficient on that valuation basis? | Surplus and funding ratio. |
| How do interest-rate changes affect assets and liabilities? | Rate conventions, bond prices, and duration. |
| How can the portfolio support the obligations? | Cash-flow matching, liability hedging, and performance-seeking allocations. |

We begin with known nominal dollar cash flows, a flat effective annual discount rate, and no taxes or transaction costs. Later sections relax the constant-rate assumption and examine hedging. Keep the liability schedule separate from the investment strategy used to finance it.

## Understanding the Time Value of Money

Compounding moves a known initial amount forward through returns; discounting values a future payment at an earlier date. From the returns chapter, wealth with no intervening cash flows evolves as:

$$
A_n=A_0\prod_{k=1}^{n}(1+R_k).
$$

If the applicable effective annual rate is a constant $r$ and time $t$ is measured in years, the accumulation factor is $(1+r)^t$. This is a valuation assumption; substituting an uncertain expected risky return does not turn a future obligation into a guaranteed investment outcome.

### Exploring Present and Future Value Concepts

At that rate, a present amount $PV$ and a payment $FV$ at time $t$ are related by:

$$
FV=PV(1+r)^t,\qquad PV=\frac{FV}{(1+r)^t}.
$$

For example, 100 today grows to 105 after one year at 5%. These are nominal currency amounts. Inflation need not be zero for the nominal compounding identity to hold; an analysis in purchasing-power units would instead pair real cash flows with a consistent real discount rate.

**Worked example.** Invest 1,000 for three years at 3% effective annually:

$$
FV=1{,}000(1.03)^3=1{,}092.727\approx1{,}092.73.
$$

```python:line-numbers
initial_amount = 1000.0
annual_rate = 0.03
years = 3
future_value = initial_amount * (1 + annual_rate) ** years
print(f"Future value after {years} years: {future_value:,.2f}")
```
<Editor id="alm-future-value" />

### Understanding the Discount Rate

The **discount rate** is $r$. The **discount factor** is the value today of one unit paid at time $t$:

$$
B(0,t)=\frac{1}{(1+r)^t}.
$$

For $t=3$ and $r=3\%$, the factor is approximately 0.915142. Thus 1,000 payable in three years has present value **915.14**, while 1,100 at the same date has present value **1,006.66**. Under the stated rate and equally certain payments, the latter is worth slightly more than 1,000 today.

```python:line-numbers
discount_factor = pok.discount(years, annual_rate).iloc[0, 0]
payment_choices = pd.Series({
    "1,000 today": 1000.0,
    "1,000 in three years": 1000 * discount_factor,
    "1,100 in three years": 1100 * discount_factor,
}, name="Present value")
print(f"Three-year discount factor: {discount_factor:.6f}")
print(payment_choices.to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:,.2f}"))
```
<Editor id="alm-discount-options" />

With positive rates, postponing the same certain payment reduces its present value. At a zero rate the factor is one; with a negative rate above −100%, it exceeds one. The toolkit takes decimal effective rates and numeric times: annual rates require times in years, not a count of months or calendar-date labels.

**Try it:** change `annual_rate` to `0.0`, then `-0.01`, in the preceding future-value cell and rerun both examples. Observe that the direction of the time-value comparison depends on the rate.

A market valuation normally uses discount factors appropriate to the payment's date, currency, and risk. A flat rate is our simplifying assumption. A higher expected return on a risky portfolio is not, by itself, a justification for discounting a promised payment at that higher rate.

### Grasping the Cumulative Present Value of Future Cash Flows

An obligation is a **schedule of payments**, not merely their sum. Let $L_i$ be the positive amount due at time $t_i$. Discount each payment to the same valuation date before adding:

$$
PV_0(L)=\sum_{i=1}^{n}B(0,t_i)L_i
=\sum_{i=1}^{n}\frac{L_i}{(1+r)^{t_i}}.
$$

For this liability analysis, outflows are recorded as positive obligations. A general net-present-value calculation can instead use signed inflows and outflows, but the sign convention must remain consistent.

**Worked schedule.** Plan A owes 8,000 at the end of year 1, 11,000 at the end of year 2, and 6,000 at the end of year 3. At a flat 5% effective annual rate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
PV_1&=\frac{8{,}000}{1.05}\approx7{,}619.05,\\
PV_2&=\frac{11{,}000}{1.05^2}\approx9{,}977.32,\\
PV_3&=\frac{6{,}000}{1.05^3}\approx5{,}183.03,\\
PV_0(L)&\approx22{,}779.40.
\end{aligned}
$$

The nominal payments sum to 25,000, but they are not all due in year 3. Discounting that total as a single three-year payment would assign the wrong timing to the first two obligations.

Enter Plan A and a second schedule, Plan B, using payment years as the row index. Apply the **same 5% valuation rate** to both so that the schedule comparison has a common basis:

### Understanding the Funding Ratio

Let $A_0$ be the current market value of assets. The **funding ratio** compares assets and liabilities at the same valuation date, while the **surplus** measures the currency difference:

$$
FR_0=\frac{A_0}{PV_0(L)},\qquad
\text{Surplus}_0=A_0-PV_0(L).
$$

- $FR_0<1$: a deficit on the stated valuation basis.
- $FR_0=1$: assets equal the liability present value.
- $FR_0>1$: a valuation surplus.

These are valuation statements, not guarantees about future asset performance. If matching default-free instruments can be purchased at the assumed discount prices, sufficient capital can finance a cash-flow-matching portfolio under the model. An existing risky portfolio can still lose value or lack cash when a payment is due.

Suppose the two plans hold current assets of 20,000 and 27,000. Keep the asset labels aligned with their liability schedules:

```python:line-numbers

The next section distinguishes effective annual, nominal, and continuously compounded rates. Their conventions must be consistent before we can model changing rates or compare hedging strategies.

### Nominal Rate and Effective Annual Interest Rate

The present-value examples used effective annual rates. A market quote may instead specify a nominal annual rate with a compounding frequency, or a continuously compounded rate. Before discounting a liability, translate the quote into the convention used by the valuation formula.

#### Annual Quotes and Periodic Rates {#short-rate-vs-long-rate-annualized}

Let $j_m$ be a **nominal annual rate compounded $m$ times per year**, where $m$ is a positive integer. The rate per compounding period is $i=j_m/m$. If interest stays invested, the effective annual rate $R$ is:

$$
1+R=\left(1+\frac{j_m}{m}\right)^m,\qquad
R=\left(1+\frac{j_m}{m}\right)^m-1.
$$

The nominal quote is an annual rate label, not the rate earned in each period. Its frequency is part of the quote. The periodic gross return must be positive: $1+j_m/m>0$.

**Hand check.** A nominal quote of 10% compounded semiannually means 5% per half-year. An initial 1,000 becomes $1{,}000(1.05)^2=1{,}102.50$, an effective annual return of **10.25%**. At a nominal 10% compounded monthly, the monthly rate is $0.10/12$, giving:

$$
R=\left(1+\frac{0.10}{12}\right)^{12}-1
\approx0.104713=10.4713\%.
$$

Compounding is not the same as making repayments. These balances assume that interest remains invested or is reinvested at the stated periodic rate, with no withdrawals or fees. If a loan amortizes or interest is paid out, value those dated cash flows separately instead of treating the principal as unchanged.

```python:line-numbers
nominal_quote = 0.10
quote_frequency = 12
deposit_principal = 1000.0
periodic_rate = nominal_quote / quote_frequency
effective_quote = pok.compounding_rate(nominal_quote, periods_per_year=quote_frequency)
end_year_balance = deposit_principal * (1 + effective_quote)
print(f"Periodic rate: {periodic_rate:.4%}")
print(f"Effective annual rate: {effective_quote:.4%}")
print(f"Balance after one year: {end_year_balance:,.2f}")
```
<Editor id="alm-nominal-compounding" />

The result is **1,104.71** after one year. The toolkit's `compounding_rate()` converts a rate quote; it is not estimating a return from historical observations.

#### Continuous Compounding

Keeping a numerical nominal quote $j$ fixed while increasing the compounding frequency gives the limit:

$$
\lim_{m\to\infty}\left(1+\frac{j}{m}\right)^{mT}=e^{jT}.
$$

A constant **continuously compounded annual rate** $r_c$ therefore gives the accumulation factor $e^{r_cT}$. Its relationship with the effective annual rate is exact:

$$
R=e^{r_c}-1,\qquad r_c=\log(1+R).
$$

At a continuously compounded rate of 10%, the effective annual rate is **10.5171%**, and 1,000 becomes **1,105.17**. That differs from a 10% nominal quote compounded monthly. Equal numerical quotes under different conventions do not necessarily describe equal economic returns.

Run the preceding monthly example first. Compare familiar frequencies in a table, then plot integer frequencies from 1 through 52 against the continuous limit:

#### Equivalent Quotes for the Same Annual Growth

To keep the economic outcome unchanged, start from an effective annual rate $R$ and solve for the equivalent quote:

$$
i_m=(1+R)^{1/m}-1,\qquad
j_m=m\left((1+R)^{1/m}-1\right),\qquad
r_c=\log(1+R).
$$

For **10% effective annual growth**, the monthly periodic rate is approximately **0.7974%**, the equivalent nominal annual quote compounded monthly is **9.5690%**, and the continuously compounded quote is **9.5310%**. All describe the same growth from 1,000 to 1,100 over one year.

```python:line-numbers

#### Connecting Rate Conventions to a Short-Rate Model

A **short rate** describes the instantaneous cash-accrual rate at a date; a **longer-maturity yield** summarizes pricing over a longer horizon. These maturity concepts are different from the choice of nominal, effective, or continuous quotation. A nominal annual quote compounded monthly is not automatically an instantaneous short rate.

If the continuously compounded short rate varies through time as $r_t$, a cash account accumulates along its realized path according to:

$$
\frac{\mathrm{Cash}_T}{\mathrm{Cash}_0}=\exp\!\left(\int_0^T r_s\,ds\right).
$$

Only when that rate remains constant does this reduce to $e^{r_cT}$. Today's short rate alone therefore does not determine a known future cash return or a long-maturity bond price. The next section introduces CIR to model the rate path and connects that process to bond valuation.

## CIR Model: Simulating Interest Rate Fluctuations

So far, discount rates have been fixed assumptions. The **Cox–Ingersoll–Ross (CIR) model** lets the short rate change through time while remaining nonnegative. It is a one-factor model: the current short rate is the state variable driving this model's bond prices. It does not represent every possible yield-curve movement or negative-rate environment.

For pricing, write the dynamics under a **risk-neutral measure**, $\mathbb{Q}$:

$$
dr_t=a(b_c-r_t)\,dt+\sigma\sqrt{r_t}\,dW_t^{\mathbb{Q}}.
$$

Here $r_t$ is a continuously compounded short rate and time is measured in years:

| Parameter | Financial role |
| --- | --- |
| $a>0$ | Mean-reversion speed, in inverse years. |
| $b_c\geq0$ | Long-run mean level of the continuous short rate. |
| $\sigma\geq0$ | Diffusion coefficient; instantaneous rate volatility is $\sigma\sqrt{r_t}$, not simply $\sigma$. |
| $r_0\geq0$ | Initial continuous short rate. |

The conditional mean is:

$$
\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}[r_t\mid r_0]
=b_c+(r_0-b_c)e^{-at}.
$$

A larger $a$ pulls the expected rate toward $b_c$ more quickly; the half-life of the expected deviation is $\log(2)/a$ years. Individual paths continue to fluctuate and need not converge monotonically.

For $\sigma>0$ and a positive initial rate, the **Feller condition** $2ab_c\geq\sigma^2$ makes zero inaccessible in the continuous model. If it fails, zero can be reached, but the CIR process remains nonnegative. With $b_c=0$, zero is absorbing. This boundary behavior differs from a naive Euler approximation, which can generate invalid negative values even when the underlying process cannot.

### Rate Conventions and Simulation

The toolkit retains an effective-annual quotation interface: its `b` and `r0` inputs are converted to $b_c=\log(1+b)$ and $r_0^{c}=\log(1+\texttt{r0})$. It returns annualized effective short-rate quotes, while the charts below convert them back to **continuous short rates**. The input `b` is the equivalent quote for $b_c$; it is not generally the mean of the transformed effective-rate distribution.

For example, an input `r0=0.05` means an initial continuous rate of approximately **4.8790%**, not 5%. These conventions are shown in the output so that the model coefficients and chart units can be checked.

The simulation uses the known **exact transition distribution** at the chosen dates. For $\sigma>0$, set:

$$
c=\frac{\sigma^2(1-e^{-a\Delta t})}{4a},\qquad
\nu=\frac{4ab_c}{\sigma^2},\qquad
\lambda=\frac{e^{-a\Delta t}r_t}{c}.
$$

Then $r_{t+\Delta t}\mid r_t$ is distributed as $c\chi'^2_{\nu}(\lambda)$, a scaled noncentral chi-squared variable. The zero-mean-level boundary case includes a point mass at zero. This avoids clipping negative Euler values and preserves the model's finite-interval distribution. When $\sigma=0$, the toolkit uses the deterministic mean-reversion solution instead.

These are **pricing-measure simulations**, not calibrated forecasts of actual future rates. Investment-outcome forecasts may need different real-world dynamics, with pricing parameters used separately to value the assets on those paths.

### Applying the CIR Model to Price Zero-Coupon Bonds

A default-free zero-coupon bond pays **one unit at maturity $T$** and no earlier coupons. Under the risk-neutral model its value is:

$$
P(t,T)=\mathbb{E}^{\mathbb{Q}}\!\left[
e^{-\int_t^T r_s\,ds}\mid r_t\right]
=A(\tau)e^{-B(\tau)r_t},\qquad \tau=T-t.
$$

For $\sigma>0$, the CIR coefficients are:

$$
\begin{aligned}
h&=\sqrt{a^2+2\sigma^2},\\
B(\tau)&=\frac{2(e^{h\tau}-1)}{2h+(a+h)(e^{h\tau}-1)},\\
A(\tau)&=\left[
\frac{2h e^{(a+h)\tau/2}}{2h+(a+h)(e^{h\tau}-1)}
\right]^{2ab_c/\sigma^2}.
\end{aligned}
$$

The rates and bond values must refer to the **same date**: price the remaining maturity using the newly observed rate, not the previous period's rate. At maturity, $\tau=0$, so $A(0)=1$, $B(0)=0$, and **$P(T,T)=1$** in every scenario.

At a fixed valuation date and maturity, a higher current continuous rate reduces price because $\partial P/\partial r_t=-B(\tau)P<0$ for $\tau>0$. Along a path, both the rate and remaining time change; the bond price need not rise monotonically toward redemption.

**Hand check.** With $\sigma=0$ and the initial rate equal to the mean level, the rate is constant. A 3% effective annual quote has continuous equivalent $\log(1.03)$. A unit payment in three years is therefore worth:

$$
P(0,3)=e^{-3\log(1.03)}=(1.03)^{-3}\approx0.915142.
$$

## Liability Hedging

Cash can have a stable short-term value and still be risky relative to a payment due many years from now. Its future reinvestment rates are unknown. A bond with the same payment date as the liability can instead lock in a terminal amount, even though its market price fluctuates before maturity.

Consider **0.75 million dollars available today** and a fixed **1 million dollar payment in ten years**. Compare investing all the starting capital in:

- A default-free zero-coupon bond maturing on the liability date.
- A cash proxy that repeatedly rolls default-free short-term bonds.

Both strategies use the same starting capital and the same simulated rate paths. Amounts are nominal dollars, expressed in millions; there are no interim liability payments, contributions, withdrawals, taxes, or transaction costs. The assets and liability use the same default-free discount curve. These assumptions make the matching mechanism visible.

### Value the Liability on the Same Curve

Let $D$ be the amount due at $T$, and let $P(t,T)$ be the price of one unit paid at $T$. The liability's present value is:

$$
L_t=D P(t,T),\qquad L_T=D.
$$

The **amount owed** remains fixed; its **present value** changes with rates and remaining time. The CIR input `b=0.03` represents a short-rate mean level through the quotation convention just established. It is neither interest added to the liability nor an assumed ten-year yield.

Run the chapter imports, then these cells in order. Simulate 500 monthly paths once and retain them for both strategies:

```python:line-numbers

### Buy the Maturity-Matched Bond

With initial capital $A_0$, the face amount purchased is $q=A_0/P(0,T)$. No additional interest is paid on top of that face amount: the zero coupon pays $q$ at maturity. Its value and funding ratio are:

$$
A_t^{\mathrm{bond}}=qP(t,T),\qquad
\mathrm{FR}_t^{\mathrm{bond}}
=\frac{qP(t,T)}{DP(t,T)}
=\frac{A_0}{DP(0,T)}.
$$

The funding ratio is **constant across dates and scenarios**. That is the hedge: asset and liability values move in the same proportion. It does not imply the ratio is at least one.

```python:line-numbers
bond_face = hedge_assets / initial_bond_price  # Face amount, in millions

Full matching requires $A_0=DP(0,T)$; then $q=D$ and the funding ratio is exactly one. Above that starting capital, investing everything in the matching bond locks in a surplus.

### Roll Short-Term Bonds as a Cash Proxy

An instantaneous short-rate quote is not a promised monthly deposit return. Instead, model a cash-like investment explicitly: at each observation date $t_k$, buy a default-free zero coupon maturing at the next date $t_{k+1}=t_k+\Delta$, where $\Delta=1/12$ year here. Its price $P(t_k,t_{k+1})$ is known when purchased. Reinvest the redemption at the next date:

$$
A_{k+1}^{\mathrm{cash}}
=\frac{A_k^{\mathrm{cash}}}{P(t_k,t_{k+1})},\qquad
R_{k,k+1}^{\mathrm{cash}}=\frac{1}{P(t_k,t_{k+1})}-1.
$$

Each holding-period return is locked in at its beginning, but **future rollover prices are unknown**. Price those short bonds with the same CIR parameters as the long bond. This is a discrete cash proxy, approaching the instantaneous cash account as the rollover interval shrinks; it does not require pretending that rates remain fixed between simulated dates.

```python:line-numbers

## Coupon-Bearing Bonds

A zero-coupon bond matches one future payment. A **coupon-bearing bond** distributes cash across several dates: regular coupons followed by the final coupon and face value. Those earlier payments matter for both liability matching and investment returns. Receiving money sooner introduces a decision about how to use or reinvest it.

Consider a default-free, fixed-rate bond purchased on a coupon date, immediately after any payment. Let $F$ be face value, $c$ the annual coupon rate, $m$ the number of coupons per year, and $T$ maturity in years. With a regular schedule and $N=mT$ whole periods:

$$
C=\frac{Fc}{m},\qquad t_i=\frac{i}{m},\qquad
\mathrm{CF}_i=\begin{cases}C,&i<N,\\ C+F,&i=N.\end{cases}
$$

Face value is the contractual redemption amount, not necessarily the amount invested. We use full, or **dirty**, prices: the value of all remaining payments. On coupon dates just after payment, accrued interest is zero, so clean and dirty prices coincide. Between coupon dates they generally differ. The examples omit default, taxes, transaction costs, and irregular stub periods.

### Construct the Payment Schedule

Use a three-year bond with face value 100 dollars, a 3% annual coupon, and semiannual payments. Each coupon is $100(0.03)/2=1.50$ dollars, not 0.015 dollars. There are five payments of 1.50 and a final payment of 101.50.

### Price the Bond from Its Yield to Maturity

The **yield to maturity (YTM)** is the cash-flow internal rate of return implied by a bond's price and promised payments. Here $y$ is a **nominal annual yield compounded at coupon frequency**. Its periodic rate is $q=y/m$, and its effective annual equivalent is $(1+y/m)^m-1$.

The time-zero price is:

$$
B_0=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\frac{\mathrm{CF}_i}{(1+q)^i}
=C\frac{1-(1+q)^{-N}}{q}+F(1+q)^{-N}.
$$

The annuity expression applies for $q\ne0$; at zero yield its limit is $NC+F$. The direct discounted sum works in both cases. At a 5% nominal YTM, discount the six payments at **2.5% per half-year**:

```python:line-numbers
coupon_ytm = 0.05
coupon_periodic_yield = coupon_ytm / coupon_terms["coupons_per_year"]
coupon_discounted = coupon_flows[0] / (1 + coupon_periodic_yield) ** coupon_flows.index
coupon_purchase_price = pok.bond_price(ytm=coupon_ytm, **coupon_terms)
print(f"Discounted payment sum: {coupon_discounted.sum():.6f}")
print(f"Toolkit price:          {coupon_purchase_price:.6f}")
print(f"Undiscounted payments:  {coupon_flows[0].sum():.2f}")
```
<Editor id="coupon-price" />

The price is approximately **94.491875 dollars**. The 109-dollar sum of payments ignores their timing; it is not an amount automatically accumulated at maturity with interest.

### Distinguish YTM from the Realized Compounded Return

YTM describes the dated promised cash flows. To turn them into a single terminal wealth amount, specify what happens to the coupons. If each received payment is held or reinvested at an effective annual rate $R_{\mathrm{reinv}}$, terminal wealth and its annualized return are:

$$
W_T=\sum_{i=1}^{N}\mathrm{CF}_i(1+R_{\mathrm{reinv}})^{T-t_i},\qquad
R_{\mathrm{realized}}=\left(\frac{W_T}{B_0}\right)^{1/T}-1.
$$

Compare zero-interest holding with reinvestment at the YTM's effective annual equivalent:

```python:line-numbers
coupon_effective_ytm = (1 + coupon_periodic_yield) ** coupon_terms["coupons_per_year"] - 1
coupon_reinvestment_rates = pd.Series({"Hold coupons at zero interest": 0.0,
                                      "Reinvest at equivalent YTM": coupon_effective_ytm})
coupon_terminal_wealth = pd.Series({
    name: (coupon_flows[0].to_numpy() * (1 + rate) ** (coupon_terms["maturity"] - coupon_times)).sum()
    for name, rate in coupon_reinvestment_rates.items()
})
coupon_realized_returns = (coupon_terminal_wealth / coupon_purchase_price) ** (1 / coupon_terms["maturity"]) - 1

                    "Annualized return (%)": coupon_realized_returns * 100}).to_string())
```
<Editor id="coupon-reinvestment" />

Holding coupons at zero interest gives **109 dollars** and approximately **4.8763%** annualized. Reinvesting at **5.0625% effective annually**, equivalent to the quoted 5% nominal YTM, gives approximately **109.581605 dollars** and **5.0625%** annualized. Simply holding to maturity does not guarantee that reinvestment rate. The scalar-price form of `bond_returns()` reports the zero-interest holding result; it does not solve for YTM.

### Price the Remaining Payments on CIR Rate Paths

Return to the short-rate model. An instantaneous short rate is **not** the YTM of a coupon bond. Instead, value each remaining payment using its own CIR zero-coupon discount factor:

$$
B_t=\sum_{t_i>t}\mathrm{CF}_iP(t,t_i).
$$

The strict inequality establishes a **post-payment convention**: any cash paid at date $t$ has left the bond and belongs to the investor separately. After the final 101.50-dollar payment, the bond has no remaining claims, so $B_T=0$. This differs from the earlier unit-zero-coupon chart, which displayed its redemption payoff of one at maturity.

### Include Distributions in Total Return

Let $D_k$ be the payment received at the end of observation interval $k$. With post-payment prices:

$$
R_k^{\mathrm{total}}=\frac{B_k+D_k}{B_{k-1}}-1.
$$

Here $D_k$ is zero in non-coupon months, 1.50 on earlier coupon dates, and **101.50 at maturity**. Count that final payment once. A percentage change in prices alone omits the distributions and would misleadingly report a 100% final-period loss when the redeemed bond's price becomes zero.

## Macaulay Duration

The coupon-bond example showed that maturity alone does not describe when an investor receives value. Some cash arrives before the final payment. **Macaulay duration** summarizes that timing as a present-value-weighted average of the payment dates.

Continue with the same three-year bond: 100 dollars face value, a 3% annual coupon paid semiannually, and a 5% nominal annual YTM. Its price is approximately **94.491875 dollars**. We value it at time zero, immediately after a coupon date, using the flat-YTM convention from the previous section.

### Weight Each Payment by Its Present Value

For payments $\mathrm{CF}_i$ at times $t_i$ measured in years, define:

$$
PV_i=\frac{\mathrm{CF}_i}{(1+y/m)^{mt_i}},\qquad
B_0=\sum_i PV_i,\qquad
w_i=\frac{PV_i}{B_0},\qquad
D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=\sum_i w_i t_i.
$$

Here $y$ is the nominal annual YTM and $m$ is coupon frequency. For nonnegative payments with positive total present value, the weights sum to one, so duration lies between the earliest and latest dates carrying positive cash flow. The denominator is the **market value of the payments**, not automatically face value.

Run the earlier coupon cash-flow and pricing cells first. Reuse their payments, dates, and discounted values to make the weights visible:

```python:line-numbers
duration_table = pd.DataFrame({
    "Payment year": coupon_times,
    "Payment": coupon_flows[0],
    "Present value": coupon_discounted,
})
duration_table["PV weight"] = duration_table["Present value"] / coupon_purchase_price
duration_table["Contribution (years)"] = duration_table["Payment year"] * duration_table["PV weight"]
duration_years = duration_table["Contribution (years)"].sum()
duration_periods = pok.mac_duration(coupon_flows, coupon_periodic_yield)
print(duration_table.to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
print(f"Weight sum: {duration_table['PV weight'].sum():.6f}")
print(f"Macaulay duration: {duration_years:.6f} years")
print(f"Toolkit result: {duration_periods:.6f} coupon periods")
```
<Editor id="duration-weights" />

The result is **2.887555 years**, or **5.775110 half-year periods**. Approximately **92.625%** of the bond's present value comes from the final payment, so the weighted-average date is close to maturity. Earlier coupons pull it below three years.

Duration is **not a payback date**. By 2.887555 years, this bond has paid only five coupons totaling 7.50 dollars; its principal is still due at year three. The duration statistic summarizes discounted timing rather than the date on which cumulative receipts recover the purchase price.

### Keep Time Units and Rate Conventions Consistent

`mac_duration()` returns duration in the units of the cash-flow index. There are two equivalent calculations:

| Cash-flow index | Discount rate passed to the helper | Result |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Coupon periods $i$ | Periodic rate $y/m$ | Duration in coupon periods; divide by $m$ for years. |
| Years $t_i=i/m$ | Effective annual rate $R=(1+y/m)^m-1$ | Duration directly in years. |

These give identical present-value weights because $(1+y/m)^{-i}=(1+R)^{-i/m}$. Dividing the dates by $m$ while passing the unconverted nominal YTM changes the discount convention.

```python:line-numbers

duration_year_flows.index = coupon_times
duration_effective_rate = (1 + coupon_periodic_yield) ** coupon_terms["coupons_per_year"] - 1

print(f"Effective annual discount rate: {duration_effective_rate:.6%}")
print(f"Coupon-period calculation: {duration_periods / coupon_terms['coupons_per_year']:.6f} years")
print(f"Year-index calculation:    {duration_from_years:.6f} years")
```
<Editor id="duration-time-units" />

Both calculations give **2.887555 years**, using **5.0625% effective annually** for the year-index calculation. Changing the units has not changed the bond. The helper supports named cash-flow columns; a single valuation returns a number, while multiple streams or rate scenarios return labeled results.

### Connect Timing to Price Sensitivity

Macaulay duration is a timing measure. To approximate sensitivity to the **nominal annual YTM** used here, convert it to **modified duration**:

$$
D_{\mathrm{mod}}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{Mac}}}{1+y/m}
=-\frac{1}{B_0}\frac{\partial B_0}{\partial y},\qquad
\frac{\Delta B}{B_0}\approx-D_{\mathrm{mod}}\Delta y.
$$

In this equation $D_{\mathrm{Mac}}$ is measured in years and $\Delta y$ is a decimal change in the nominal annual quote. **One basis point is 0.0001**; a move from 5% to 5.5% is +50 bp, or $\Delta y=0.005$. Reprice the same payments at the same valuation date to compare the local approximation with the exact result:

```python:line-numbers
duration_modified = duration_years / (1 + coupon_periodic_yield)
duration_shifts_bps = np.array([-100, -50, -1, 0, 1, 50, 100])
duration_repriced = np.array([
    pok.bond_price(ytm=coupon_ytm + shift / 10000, **coupon_terms)
    for shift in duration_shifts_bps
])
duration_sensitivity = pd.DataFrame({
    "Yield change (bp)": duration_shifts_bps,
    "Exact price change (%)": (duration_repriced / coupon_purchase_price - 1) * 100,
    "Duration approximation (%)": -duration_modified * duration_shifts_bps / 100,
})
print(f"Modified duration: {duration_modified:.6f} years")
print(duration_sensitivity.to_string(index=False, float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
```
<Editor id="duration-sensitivity" />

Modified duration is approximately **2.817127 years**. For +50 bp, it predicts **−1.408563%**, compared with an exact **−1.396769%** price change. The approximation captures the local slope; the curved price–yield relationship creates a gap for finite changes. Even a zero coupon has a curved price–yield relationship, although its Macaulay duration is always its maturity.

This is sensitivity to a single YTM quote with fixed payments. It does not by itself measure every movement of a non-flat yield curve or the CIR short-rate factor. That distinction matters when duration is later used for liability hedging.

## Liability Driven Investing (LDI)

### Creating Duration-Matched Portfolios

The earlier zero-coupon hedge matched one liability payment exactly. With several obligations and a limited choice of bonds, we may instead match their **present-value-weighted timing**. This can reduce sensitivity to small parallel changes in rates, even when the individual payment dates differ.

Keep two questions separate: **Are the assets sufficient at today's prices?** And **do asset and liability values respond similarly to a rate change?** We will answer both for 130,000 dollars of initial capital and two 100,000-dollar obligations due in years 10 and 12.

#### Value the Liabilities on a Common Curve

Use a flat **4% effective annual discount rate**, with all cash-flow dates measured in years. Assets and liabilities share this curve; payments are fixed and default-free. Fractional bond holdings are allowed, and transaction costs are omitted. Run the chapter imports before these cells.

```python:line-numbers
ldi_assets, ldi_rate = 130000.0, 0.04
ldi_liabilities = pd.DataFrame({"Liability": [100000.0, 100000.0]}, index=[10, 12])
ldi_liability_pv = float(pok.present_value(ldi_liabilities, ldi_rate).iloc[0])
ldi_liability_duration = pok.mac_duration(ldi_liabilities, ldi_rate)
print(f"Liability PV: {ldi_liability_pv:,.2f}")
print(f"Liability duration: {ldi_liability_duration:.6f} years")
print(f"Initial funding ratio: {ldi_assets / ldi_liability_pv:.6f}")
print(f"PV surplus / deficit: {ldi_assets - ldi_liability_pv:,.2f}")
```
<Editor id="ldi-liabilities" />

The liability PV is **130,016.12 dollars**, and its duration is **10.960799 years**. The available capital is therefore **16.12 dollars short**, with a funding ratio of approximately **0.999876**. Rounding that ratio to 1 would hide the initial deficit.

#### Combine Bonds Using Market-Value Weights

The available bonds have 1,000-dollar face values, 5% annual coupons, and maturities of 10 and 20 years. Both initially pay annually, so coupon-period indices already denote years. Price their payments on the same curve used for the liabilities:

```python:line-numbers

ldi_prices = pok.present_value(ldi_bond_flows, ldi_rate)
ldi_durations = pok.mac_duration(ldi_bond_flows, ldi_rate)
print(pd.DataFrame({"Price": ldi_prices, "Duration (years)": ldi_durations}).to_string())
```
<Editor id="ldi-bonds" />

The short bond costs approximately **1,081.108958** dollars and has duration **8.190899 years**. The long bond costs **1,135.903263** dollars and has duration **13.544718 years**. The liability duration lies between them.

If $w_s$ is the fraction of **current asset market value** invested in the short bond, then:

$$
D_A=w_sD_s+(1-w_s)D_l,\qquad
w_s=\frac{D_L-D_l}{D_s-D_l},\qquad w_l=1-w_s.
$$

These are market-value weights, not fractions of the bond count or face value. For two distinct asset durations, a long-only match requires the liability duration to lie between them. A target outside that interval would require a short position or a different set of instruments; it cannot be obtained by clipping an invalid weight into the interval. If both asset durations are equal, changing their weights cannot change portfolio duration, and the formula does not identify a unique allocation.

```python:line-numbers
ldi_weights = pd.Series(
    pok.duration_match_weight(ldi_durations["Short"], ldi_durations["Long"], ldi_liability_duration),
    index=ldi_prices.index,
)
ldi_units = ldi_weights * ldi_assets / ldi_prices
ldi_allocation = pd.DataFrame({"Market-value weight": ldi_weights,
                               "Amount invested": ldi_weights * ldi_assets,
                               "Bond quantity": ldi_units})
print(ldi_allocation.to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
```
<Editor id="ldi-weights" />

The allocation is approximately **48.2631% short** and **51.7369% long**, buying **58.034872** and **59.211011** bonds respectively. The purchase rule is $q_j=w_jA_0/B_j$, so the quantities reconcile to the available budget.

The portfolio's coupons and redemptions do not coincide with the two 100,000-dollar obligations. Meeting those obligations may involve reinvesting earlier receipts and selling remaining bonds; duration matching is not cash-flow matching.

#### Reprice Fixed Holdings Under Parallel Rate Changes

Let $A(R)$ and $L(R)$ denote asset and liability PVs under a common effective annual rate $R$. The funding ratio is $F(R)=A(R)/L(R)$. From the preceding duration calculation:

$$
\frac{d\log F}{dR}=-\frac{D_A-D_L}{1+R}.
$$

Matching Macaulay durations on this common curve makes the funding ratio **locally flat**, even if it starts below one. For the dollar surplus $S=A-L$, however:

$$
\frac{dS}{dR}=\frac{-AD_A+LD_L}{1+R}.
$$

Matching both **present value** and **duration** makes the initial surplus and its first derivative zero. Duration matching alone does not satisfy both conditions.

Compare short-only, long-only, and matched portfolios purchased with the same 130,000-dollar budget. Keep their bond quantities fixed as the discount rate changes; recomputing the holdings at each rate would describe different portfolios.

```python:line-numbers

We have now constructed a liability-oriented bond portfolio and identified what its duration match protects against. Next, we introduce a performance-seeking portfolio and examine how allocating between the two changes the investment outcome.

### Integrating Performance-Seeking Portfolio (PSP) with Liability-Hedging Portfolio (LHP)

An LDI portfolio can separate a **performance-seeking portfolio (PSP)**, intended to earn risk premia, from a **liability-hedging portfolio (LHP)**, designed around obligations. Combining them creates a trade-off: more growth exposure also creates more uncertainty about the resources available to pay liabilities.

The preceding section constructed a duration-matched bond portfolio. Here we isolate the mechanics of combining sleeves: an equity total-return index and an illustrative bond sleeve. The bond sleeve is a **candidate LHP**, not a demonstrated hedge of the earlier liability schedule. Its 60/40 weights are assumptions rather than duration-matching results. We compare wealth per unit invested and a terminal threshold; these are not funding ratios.

Use a **ten-year observation horizon**, 1,000 scenarios, and semiannual observations and rebalancing. The long bond still has ten years remaining when the experiment ends.

##### Evaluate Terminal Outcomes

Use a terminal threshold $f=0.8$ per unit initially invested. For scenario $j$, define $I_j=\mathbf{1}\{W_T^{(j)}<f\}$ and $L_j=\max(f-W_T^{(j)},0)$. The observed shortfall fraction is $\sum_j I_j/N$. The **conditional shortfall** averages $L_j$ only across misses, while the **unconditional mean shortfall** averages it across every scenario. These are shortfalls relative to a fixed wealth threshold, not percentile-based CVaR.

```python:line-numbers

    "Prob breach": "Terminal shortfalls (%)", "Exp shortfall": "Conditional shortfall",

#### Glide Path Weight Allocation Strategy

A **glide path** changes the target allocation according to a schedule. Here, the stock share declines from 80% to 20%, with the remainder in the same illustrative bond sleeve. The strategy still rebalances periodically, but its target changes with time rather than staying at 70/30. It does not respond to gains, losses, or the funding ratio.

##### Specify When Each Weight Applies

For $N\geq2$ equally spaced return intervals, number the allocation decisions $i=0,\ldots,N-1$. Set the stock weight **before** earning the corresponding return:

$$
w_i=w_{\mathrm{start}}+(w_{\mathrm{end}}-w_{\mathrm{start}})\frac{i}{N-1},\qquad
r_{i+1}^{\mathrm{glide}}=w_i r_{i+1}^{\mathrm{stock}}+(1-w_i)r_{i+1}^{\mathrm{bond}}.
$$

This convention applies the end target to the **last return interval**. In the ten-year semiannual example there are 20 decisions: 80% is set at year zero and 20% at year **9.5**, before the return ending at year ten. There is no additional earned return or required rebalance at the horizon. With only one interval, the helper uses the start target alone.

Run the preceding fixed-mix examples first. Reuse their aligned simple-return frames without drawing new scenarios:

#### Integrating Floor Considerations with Performance-Seeking and Liability-Hedging Portfolios

A glide path responds to the calendar. A **floor-aware allocator** responds to the resources available above a specified obligation. Return to a default-free zero-coupon bond that matures on the liability date: its value tracks the current cost of meeting that terminal obligation.

Normalize initial wealth to $W_0=1$ and require an amount $f$ at year $T$. If the unit bond price is $P_t=P(t,T)$, the floor's **present value** is:

$$
F_t=fP_t,\qquad P_T=1,\qquad F_T=f.
$$

The amount due is fixed, but its current value moves with rates and time. A terminal promise of 0.8 is therefore different from requiring wealth to stay above 0.8 at every earlier date.

Use a fresh ten-year, monthly experiment with 1,000 paired scenarios. Retain the preceding assumption that CIR scenario dynamics equal pricing dynamics, with zero modeled short-rate risk premium and independent equity shocks. The stock total-return GBM now has drift 7% and volatility **15%**. These remain uncalibrated assumptions. Run the chapter imports first:

    n_years=n_years, n_scenarios=n_scenarios, mu=0.07, sigma=0.15,

#### Floor Allocator

At each decision date, calculate the cushion $C_t=W_t-F_t$ and allocate a multiple of it to the PSP. With no borrowing or short sales, the risky amount and weight are:

$$
E_t=\min\!\left(W_t,\max(mC_t,0)\right),\qquad
w_t=\frac{E_t}{W_t},\qquad
W_{t+\Delta}=E_tG^{S}_{t+\Delta}+(W_t-E_t)G^{B}_{t+\Delta},
$$

where $G^S=1+r^{\mathrm{stock}}$ and $G^B=P_{t+\Delta}/P_t$. Decisions use $W_t$ and $P_t$ **before** the interval's returns occur. Unlike the calendar rule, this allocation responds to the remaining cushion. It is a policy rule, not an optimizer that maximizes performance.

Run multipliers 1, 3, and 5 on exactly the same paths. `ldi_floor_backtest()` records wealth and floor values at all observation dates, with weights and cushions indexed by the return interval they precede:

##### What the Reserve Argument Establishes

If the promise is initially funded, $0\leq m\leq1$ keeps the bond investment at least as large as the floor PV. For $m=1$, $E_t=C_t$, so:

$$
W_{t+\Delta}-F_{t+\Delta}=C_tG^S_{t+\Delta}\geq0.
$$

This relies on nonnegative stock values, a default-free bond matching the payment date, and no costs or withdrawals. In fact, with $m=1$ the strategy is equivalent to buying $f$ units of the bond and investing the remaining initial cushion in the stock total-return index:

$$
W_t=fP_t+(1-fP_0)\frac{S_t}{S_0}.
$$

That is a conditional funding argument, not a conclusion drawn from observing zero failures in a simulation. If $fP_0>1$, the reserve cannot be purchased with the available capital.

For $m>1$, part of the reserve is exposed to stocks. When the exposure is uncapped and the cushion is positive:

$$
C_{t+\Delta}=C_tG^B_{t+\Delta}
\left[1+m\left(\frac{G^S_{t+\Delta}}{G^B_{t+\Delta}}-1\right)\right].
$$

The cushion becomes negative if the stock's return **relative to the hedging bond** is below $-1/m$. This is not generally an absolute stock-loss threshold because the bond price also moves. The capped case follows the full wealth equation above. Once wealth falls below the floor PV, the rule assigns zero to stocks; switching into the bond preserves the funding deficit rather than repairing it.

#### Drawdown Allocator

The preceding floor protected a fixed amount due at a future date. A **drawdown rule** instead refers to losses from the portfolio's own previous wealth peak. Those objectives differ: an investor can finish above the starting capital after losing a substantial fraction of an earlier gain.

For a requested drawdown limit $d$, define the observed high-water mark and current reference floor:

$$
H_t=\max(W_0,\ldots,W_t),\qquad F_t=(1-d)H_t,\qquad
\mathrm{DD}_t=\frac{W_t}{H_t}-1.
$$

Include initial wealth in the peak. Drawdowns are zero at a high-water mark and negative below it; a breach of the requested limit means $\mathrm{DD}_t<-d$. The floor rises with new peaks and does not fall when wealth declines. It is not discounted using a bond price, because it is a reference for **current nominal wealth**, not a fixed terminal payment.

The toolkit retains the rule $m=1/d$ and sets the beginning-period stock weight to:

$$
w_t=\min\!\left(1,\max\!\left(\frac{1}{d}\frac{W_t-F_t}{W_t},0\right)\right).
$$

Choose this weight before earning the next return. Only afterward update wealth, the high-water mark, and the floor for the following decision. Each strategy uses its **own** wealth peak, not the stock index's peak.

At a peak, $W_t=H_t$, so $w_t=(1/d)d=1$. Thus this rule starts **100% in stocks**, even when the requested limit is tightened. It can exceed the limit before the next rebalance; `maxdd` is a policy parameter, not a guarantee.

## Additional Insights: Modified Duration

The earlier duration example used a nominal annual YTM compounded at coupon frequency. Close the chapter by repeating the sensitivity calculation with a **continuously compounded annual yield**, denoted $z$. The bond's value does not depend on how an equivalent rate is quoted, but the numerical derivative with respect to that quote does.

Hold the valuation date and promised cash flows fixed. For payments $c_i$ at times $t_i$ measured in years, a flat continuous yield gives:

$$
B(z)=\sum_i c_i e^{-zt_i},\qquad
w_i=\frac{c_i e^{-zt_i}}{B(z)},\qquad
D_{\mathrm{Mac}}=\sum_i t_iw_i.
$$

### Duration Revisited {#duration-revisited}

Differentiate with respect to the **annual continuous quote**, not a coupon-period rate:

$$
\frac{\partial B}{\partial z}=-\sum_i t_i c_i e^{-zt_i}
=-B D_{\mathrm{Mac}},\qquad
D_{\mathrm{mod}}^{(z)}=-\frac{1}{B}\frac{\partial B}{\partial z}=D_{\mathrm{Mac}}.
$$

These derivative identities are **exact**. For a finite yield change, however, the duration estimate is only first order:

$$
\Delta B=B(z+\Delta z)-B(z)\approx-BD_{\mathrm{Mac}}\Delta z.
$$

### A Continuous-Yield Worked Example

Use a three-year bond with face value 100 dollars and a 10% annual coupon paid semiannually. It pays five coupons of 5 dollars and a final payment of 105 dollars. The discount yield is **12% continuously compounded annually**; the coupon rate still determines the contractual payment amounts.

Run the chapter imports, then price the dated payments directly:

```python:line-numbers
modified_terms = {"principal": 100, "maturity": 3, "coupon_rate": 0.10, "coupons_per_year": 2}
continuous_yield = 0.12
modified_flows = pok.bond_cash_flows(**modified_terms)
modified_times = modified_flows.index.to_numpy() / modified_terms["coupons_per_year"]
modified_pvs = modified_flows[0].to_numpy() * np.exp(-continuous_yield * modified_times)
modified_price = modified_pvs.sum()
modified_weights = modified_pvs / modified_price
modified_mac = (modified_times * modified_weights).sum()
print(pd.DataFrame({"Payment year": modified_times, "Payment": modified_flows[0].to_numpy(),
                    "PV": modified_pvs, "PV weight": modified_weights,
                    "Duration contribution": modified_times * modified_weights}).to_string(index=False))
print(f"Price: {modified_price:.6f}; Macaulay duration: {modified_mac:.6f} years")
```
<Editor id="modified-duration-pricing" />

The price is approximately **94.213021 dollars**, and Macaulay duration is **2.653010 years**. Under this continuous quotation, the same 2.653010 is the relative price sensitivity to a unit change in the annual yield.

### Dollar Sensitivity and Basis Points

Here **dollar duration** means $-\partial B/\partial z=BD_{\mathrm{Mac}}$, per unit of the decimal annual quote. Define **DV01** as the positive first-order price loss for a one-basis-point yield increase:

$$
\mathrm{DV01}=BD_{\mathrm{Mac}}\,10^{-4}.
$$

One bp is **0.0001**, or **0.01 percentage point**. A move from 12% to 12.1% is +10 bp, so $\Delta z=0.001$, not 0.10. Confirm the analytical slope and toolkit calculations:

```python:line-numbers
modified_slope = -modified_price * modified_mac
modified_dv01 = -modified_slope * 1e-4
modified_measures = pok.bond_sensitivity(ytm=continuous_yield, quote="continuous", **modified_terms)
print(f"Price derivative per unit of continuous yield: {modified_slope:.6f}")
print(f"DV01: {modified_dv01:.6f} dollars per 100 face value")
print(pd.Series(modified_measures).to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
```
<Editor id="modified-duration-sensitivity" />

The derivative is approximately **−249.948089 dollars per unit yield change**, and DV01 is **0.024995 dollars**. These are local sensitivities; DV01 is not an exact finite price difference. Changing face value scales price and dollar sensitivity, while duration is unchanged.

### Compare Exact Repricing with the Tangent

Revalue the same cash flows at the changed yield. Keep the original price and duration as the approximation's base rather than overwriting them:

```python:line-numbers
modified_shifts_bps = np.array([-100, -10, -1, 0, 1, 10, 100])
modified_yield_changes = modified_shifts_bps / 10000
modified_exact_prices = (modified_flows[0].to_numpy() * np.exp(
    -(continuous_yield + modified_yield_changes[:, None]) * modified_times,
)).sum(axis=1)
modified_linear_prices = modified_price + modified_slope * modified_yield_changes
modified_repricing = pd.DataFrame({
    "Exact price": modified_exact_prices, "Duration estimate": modified_linear_prices,
    "Exact minus estimate": modified_exact_prices - modified_linear_prices,
}, index=pd.Index(modified_shifts_bps, name="Continuous yield change (bp)"))
print(modified_repricing.to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
```
<Editor id="modified-duration-repricing" />

For +10 bp, duration predicts a price change of **−0.249948 dollars** and a new price of **93.963072**. Exact repricing gives **93.963429**, an error of approximately **+0.000356 dollars**. Agreement to three decimal places in this example is not a general accuracy guarantee.

For positive future cash flows, $\partial^2B/\partial z^2=\sum_i t_i^2c_i e^{-zt_i}>0$. The helper reports normalized **convexity**, $\mathcal C_z=B^{-1}\partial^2B/\partial z^2=\sum_i t_i^2w_i$, in years squared. This curvature explains why the exact price lies above its tangent for either sign of a nonzero yield shift. The first-order approximation becomes less accurate as the move grows. Even a zero coupon has a curved exponential price–yield relationship.

### Reconcile Continuous and Nominal Quotes

The equivalent nominal annual yield compounded $m$ times a year is $y_m=m(e^{z/m}-1)$, and the effective annual rate is $R=e^z-1$. They give the same discount factors and price. The chain rule changes the sensitivity measure:

$$
z=m\log(1+y_m/m),\qquad
D_{\mathrm{mod}}^{(y_m)}=\frac{D_{\mathrm{Mac}}}{1+y_m/m}.
$$

Thus the earlier nominal-yield formula and the continuous-yield result agree; they differentiate with respect to **different quote variables**. Macaulay duration remains the same for equivalent valuations.

```python:line-numbers
modified_frequency = modified_terms["coupons_per_year"]
modified_nominal_quote = modified_frequency * np.expm1(continuous_yield / modified_frequency)
modified_effective_quote = np.expm1(continuous_yield)
modified_year_flows = modified_flows.copy()
modified_year_flows.index = modified_times
modified_quote_prices = pd.Series({
    "Continuous discounting": modified_price,
    "Equivalent nominal quote": pok.bond_price(ytm=modified_nominal_quote, **modified_terms),
    "Equivalent effective quote": pok.present_value(modified_year_flows, modified_effective_quote).iloc[0],
})
modified_nominal_measures = pok.bond_sensitivity(ytm=modified_nominal_quote, quote="nominal", **modified_terms)
modified_shifted_nominal = modified_frequency * np.expm1((continuous_yield + 0.001) / modified_frequency)
print(modified_quote_prices.to_string(float_format=lambda value: f"{value:.6f}"))
print(f"Equivalent nominal annual quote: {modified_nominal_quote:.6%}")
print(f"Nominal-quote modified duration: {modified_nominal_measures['Modified duration']:.6f}")
print(f"A continuous +10 bp move maps to {(modified_shifted_nominal - modified_nominal_quote) * 10000:.6f} nominal bp")
```
<Editor id="modified-duration-quotes" />

All three prices are **94.213021**. The equivalent semiannual nominal quote is approximately **12.367309%**, not 12%. A continuous +10 bp move corresponds to approximately **+10.621020 nominal bp** here. Applying the same numeric 10-bp change to both quotation systems would describe different repricings.
